"Vibe coding" — building software by describing what you want and letting AI figure out the details—has created an explosion of new tools. In this lesson, we'll map the landscape into three categories (AI app builders, AI prototyping tools, and AI development tools), give you a framework for choosing the right tool based on what you're building, and discuss when to switch tools as your needs and skills grow.

## Welcome to the era of vibe coding

In a short amount of time, the number of AI-powered coding tools has proliferated. We are now living in a new era of "vibe coding"—a term coined by AI researcher Andrej Karpathy to describe a style of building software where you describe what you want in plain language and let the AI figure out the details. You're not writing precise code; you're guiding the direction and going with what feels right. This means non-technical people can now build surprisingly complicated software.

For product managers, designers, and other non-technical members of the product development team, vibe coding means you can build proof of concepts and prototypes without needing support from the engineering team—who are likely focused on writing production software.

In the previous lesson, we walked through why this is such a fundamental shift for the product development lifecycle. It used to be that we could only afford to build working software at the end of the process, or in very special circumstances where a working prototype was absolutely required. Now we can use working software to explore ideas, make requirements crystal clear, align teams, and validate with customers.

## Why we focus on Reforge Build

In this course, we will primarily focus on Reforge Build because it has been purpose-built for product builders who want to integrate AI prototyping into their workflow.

That said, AI prototyping is one of many use cases for vibe coding and AI-powered development tools. It's helpful to understand this landscape so you know when to reach for which tool—and understand why people on your team might be using a particular product.

## Understanding the landscape

The landscape of tools is constantly changing. This is exciting because it means we have more powerful tools to work with every day. But it can also be overwhelming.

Dan Olsen has cataloged the landscape of tools into a spectrum organized primarily by the tool's target user—with tools designed for less technical users on the left and tools designed for more technical users, like professional developers, on the right:

These tools are powerful, and there's lots of overlap in their capabilities as well as unique features that make each stand out for specific use cases.

That said, we can roughly bucket them into three categories:

### 1. AI app & website builders

**Examples**: Bolt, v0, Lovable, Replit, Base44, Google AI Studio, Dreamflow

The most popular coding tools, like **Lovable** and **Bolt**, fall into this category. These tools are designed primarily for people going from 0 to 1 who want to build and deploy a complete application.

For example, Lovable’s tagline is: “Create apps and websites by chatting with AI”. One of Lovable’s best features is Lovable Cloud, a drop-in system that enables users to add more complicated features, like the ability to save data to a database, add user authentication, and connect with other services like OpenAI for LLM integration and Stripe for payments. Lovable also features the ability to deploy to a production server, setup a custom domain, and access live analytics. Bolt has a comparable set of features and focus to Lovable.

Other tools in this category have different strengths. **v0** (by Vercel) produces high-quality UI and excellent frontend code—it's often a first step for prototyping UI before bringing that code into one of the AI development tools below to build a production app. **Dreamflow** specializes in mobile app development, using Flutter to generate native iOS and Android applications.

Although products in this category can be used for AI prototyping, they are not designed for it. They typically have limited ability to use established design systems, tie into your company's product and strategic context, and connect with internal APIs or datasets.

### 2. AI prototyping tools

**Examples**: Reforge Build, Magic Patterns, Figma Make, Alloy

These tools are purpose-built for product teams who need to prototype and validate ideas quickly—turning concepts into working software that helps them brainstorm ideas, align teams, and validate concepts with customers.

For example, Reforge Build solves one of the biggest problems PMs face when prototyping: context. With **Company Context**, Build understands your company's branding and design systems. **Project Context** lets you share PRDs, OKRs, one-pagers, and screenshots to customize specific prototypes, while **Project Rules** let you set clear guidelines for what is and isn't desired. Reforge Build also integrates with Figma and offers a browser extension that can capture any screen or flow, instantly turning your existing product into an editable prototype.

The key distinction of this category is that prototypes are designed to be **decision-making tools, not production software**. A prototype isn't an end deliverable—it's a tool for decision-making, communication, and validation. These tools are less focused on production deployments, keeping the focus on quickly testing interaction patterns and gathering feedback rather than building production-ready applications.

### 3. AI development tools

**Examples**: Cursor, Claude Code, Codex, Google Antigravity, Windsurf

These tools are designed for technical users and professional developers. The most notable are Cursor and Claude Code, both of which have developed truly remarkable agentic capabilities over the past year.

Cursor is an IDE, or integrated development environment, designed from the ground up to be AI-first. An IDE is similar in many ways to Notion, offering the ability to edit text files, organize source code, build projects, validate code, and test and iterate on working software.

For many years, the most popular IDE has been VS Code (part of Microsoft’s Visual Studio suite). Microsoft added AI-powered coding to VS Code through an extension called GitHub Copilot. This was a revolution for many developers, but the experience felt somewhat bolted-on. Cursor, which is based on the VS Code codebase, was designed with AI integrated more deeply into the environment. This more streamlined experience has quickly gained popularity—as has Cursor's ability to use different AI models like Claude, Gemini, or GPT.

Claude Code takes a different approach as a command-line tool that can work with any code editor, including Cursor. It's the most technical option to work with, but that also affords it significant power—including the ability to leverage hundreds of different programs and utilities available from your computer's terminal. This approach, combined with the excellent quality of Claude's coding models, has given Claude Code a reputation as one of the most powerful ways to build software with AI assistance. Today, many professional developers use both: Cursor as their development environment and Claude Code as their AI-powered coding assistant.

For prototyping purposes, AI development tools may be overkill and can slow you down due to technical complexity. However, when you need additional power, these tools can take your prototypes up a notch. It's also worth noting that code is portable _into_ these tools from the AI app builders and prototyping tools we've discussed, making it possible to “graduate” if you need the additional power. But, the reverse is not true. Once code is in a development tool, it's usually difficult to port back to simpler tools.

## How to choose your tool

With so many options, how do you know which tool to reach for? Here's a simple framework based on three factors:

## 1. What you're trying to build

Different goals call for different tools:

- **Quick concept exploration** — Use AI prototyping tools. They're optimized for speed and iteration, not production readiness.
    
- **A complete application you'll actually deploy** — Use AI app builders. They include the infrastructure for databases, authentication, and hosting.
    
- **A complex feature or integration with existing code** — Use AI development tools. They can work with your existing codebase and handle sophisticated requirements.
    

## 2. Your context requirements

How much does the prototype need to reflect your company's reality?

- **Generic exploration** — Any tool works. You're testing concepts, not brand alignment.
    
- **Needs to look like your product** — Use AI prototyping tools with design system support (like Reforge Build) or be prepared to spend significant time customizing in other tools.
    
- **Needs to maintain project context** — If your prototypes need to understand your goals, metrics, PRDs, or other strategic context, use a tool with built-in context management. Reforge Build excels here. You can share company and project level context, and you can even use the Reforge Extension to capture any screen or flow from your existing product.
    
- **Needs to connect to real data or APIs** — Use a tool designed to work with your internal systems and APIs.
    

## 3. Your technical comfort level

Start where you are—and know that you can always grow from here:

- **Non-technical** — You've never written code and don't want to start now. Stick with AI app builders (Lovable, Bolt) or AI prototyping tools (Reforge Build). These tools handle the complexity for you.
    
- **Technical** — You can write code or have in the past. AI development tools (Cursor, Claude Code) become viable options when you need more power or control.
    

**Become technically curious.** You can get more out of any vibe coding tool by becoming more technically savvy. This means getting comfortable looking at code even if you can't write it from scratch. You'll get the most out of your tool and understand what's being generated, which helps with prompting and debugging.

In a future lesson, we're going to look at some principles of software engineering that you can get familiar with to help you be more comfortable with the underlying code generation work that these tools are doing.

## The quick decision guide

|If you need...|Reach for...|
|---|---|
|Fast concept exploration with your design system|AI prototyping tools (Reforge Build)|
|A deployable app or website|AI app builders (Lovable, Bolt)|
|High-quality front-end UI/code|v0|
|A native mobile app|Dreamflow|
|Maximum power and flexibility|AI development tools (Cursor + Claude Code)|

## Switching between tools

As you develop your prototyping skills and take on more complicated tasks, you may find yourself hitting the limits of your tool. Think of it like graduating—each level unlocks new capabilities while building on what you've learned.

### Stage 1: Chat interfaces (ChatGPT, Claude)

Most people start here. You can generate code snippets, get explanations, and even create full scripts. But you'll need to manually copy and paste code to get it running—there's no preview or iteration environment. This is where many people discover AI can write code and start wondering what else is possible.

### Stage 2: AI app builders (Lovable, Bolt, v0)

The first real "vibe coding" experience. You describe what you want, and you get a working application you can see and interact with. No setup required. Great for going from zero to something tangible. The limitation: these tools are designed for building complete apps, not for rapid prototyping within a product team's workflow.

### Stage 3: AI prototyping tools (Reforge Build)

Purpose-built for product teams. This is where prototyping becomes a professional skill rather than a novelty. Design system integration, project context, and collaboration features make these tools suitable for real product development work. You're no longer just building demos—you're building decision-making tools.

### Stage 4: AI development tools (Cursor, Claude Code)

The most powerful option—and the most technical. These tools can handle complex, production-grade work. Many professional developers now use them daily. For prototyping purposes, you'll reach for these when you hit the limits of simpler tools or need to integrate with existing codebases.

## You don't have to climb the whole ladder

Most product managers and designers will find their sweet spot at Stage 2 or 3. That's not a limitation—it's focus. The goal isn't to become a developer. The goal is to use prototyping effectively in your product development process.

That said, understanding what's possible at each stage helps you:

- Know when to "graduate" to a more powerful tool
    
- Communicate effectively with engineers who use these tools
    
- Make informed decisions about when a prototype should become production code
    

## Code portability: a one-way street

One important principle: **code flows down the evolution path, not up.**

You can take code from Reforge Build, Lovable, or v0 and bring it into Cursor or Claude Code to continue development. This is a common pattern—prototype quickly in a simpler tool, then hand off to engineering (or graduate the code yourself) when the prototype is validated.

But the reverse doesn't work well. Once code is in a professional development environment with its own structure, dependencies, and complexity, it's difficult to bring it back into the simpler tools. Plan accordingly.
