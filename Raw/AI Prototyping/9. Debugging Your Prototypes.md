# 



Even with AI tools, prototypes don't always work perfectly on the first try. In this lesson, you'll learn systematic approaches to debugging issues in your prototypes—from understanding error messages to using browser developer tools. We'll cover key debugging techniques, including forking projects to test different approaches, using prompt replay to refine your instructions, and recognizing when it's time to graduate to a more powerful tool as your prototype grows in complexity.

Even the best prompts don’t always produce a perfect prototype on the first try. Something will look wrong. A button won’t work. Data will show up in the wrong place—or won’t show up at all. This is completely normal. It happens to everyone, from first-time prototypers to experienced engineers.

The good news: the same AI that built your prototype can also fix it. Your job isn’t to debug the code yourself—it’s to help the AI understand what’s wrong. That means describing problems clearly, providing evidence, and knowing when to try a different approach instead of pushing the AI in circles.

This lesson gives you a systematic debugging toolkit—a set of techniques ordered from simplest to most advanced. When something goes wrong, start at the top and work your way down.

## 

## The debugging ladder

Think of debugging as a ladder. Start with the simplest fix and escalate only if it doesn’t work. Most issues resolve in the first few rungs.

![](https://cdn5.dcbstatic.com/files/r/e/reforge_docebosaas_com/1790290800/UoviUdyE0A9VxxSvgzh7GQ/scorm/4c7a9d8af9f1c9ff6100277eff0737e7d11e6a117404ad4be1299da44cd1b846/scormcontent/assets/debugging-ladder-no-subtex.svg)

You won’t need every rung for every issue. A quick reload fixes more problems than you’d expect. But when you do need to escalate, knowing the sequence keeps you from jumping to drastic measures too early—or spinning your wheels too long on something that needs a fresh start.

## 

## Start with the basics

Before reaching for advanced techniques, try the simple things. These resolve a surprising number of issues and take just seconds.

### 

## Refresh the prototype

Most prototyping tools have a reload or refresh button within the preview pane. This forces the tool to rebuild your prototype from the current code. It’s the equivalent of turning it off and on again—and it works more often than you’d think, particularly when the preview has gotten out of sync with the code.

![](https://cdn5.dcbstatic.com/files/r/e/reforge_docebosaas_com/1790290800/UoviUdyE0A9VxxSvgzh7GQ/scorm/4c7a9d8af9f1c9ff6100277eff0737e7d11e6a117404ad4be1299da44cd1b846/scormcontent/assets/refresh_preview.jpg)

### 

## Reload the browser window

If refreshing the preview doesn’t help, try reloading the entire browser window. This clears any stale state the browser might be holding in memory. As we discussed in [Software Architecture for Non-Technical Builders(opens in a new tab)](https://edu.reforge.com/learn/courses/7165/ai-prototyping/lessons/14115:6755/prototyping-fundamentals), data stored in browser memory is wiped on refresh—which can sometimes clear out a corrupted state that’s causing odd behavior.

### 

## Describe the problem to the AI

If the basics don’t fix it, the next step is to tell the AI what’s going wrong. This is the single most important debugging skill, and it is about communication, not code.

Be specific. There’s a big difference between “it’s broken” and “when I click the Day 2 tab, the activities don’t change—it still shows Day 1’s items.” The more precisely you describe three things—what you expected, what actually happened, and what you were doing when it broke—the more likely the AI will fix it in one attempt.

If the issue is visual—something looks wrong on screen—include a screenshot. All major prototyping tools accept image uploads in their chat. A screenshot paired with a description like “the button in the top right is cut off on mobile” is far more effective than either alone.

![](https://cdn5.dcbstatic.com/files/r/e/reforge_docebosaas_com/1790290800/UoviUdyE0A9VxxSvgzh7GQ/scorm/4c7a9d8af9f1c9ff6100277eff0737e7d11e6a117404ad4be1299da44cd1b846/scormcontent/assets/enter_selection_mode.png)

Some tools also have features that make this easier. Reforge has an “Enter selection mode” feature and Lovable has a “Select Element” feature that lets you click on a specific UI element in the preview to reference it directly in your message. This saves you from having to describe where the element is located.

### How issues show up

Not all problems look the same, and recognizing which kind you’re dealing with points you toward the right next step.

- **Visual issues** are things that look wrong—a color is off, spacing is uneven, text is overlapping, an image is stretched. The feature works, it just doesn’t look the way you want. These rarely produce error messages, so the best approach is to describe what you see to the AI as specifically as possible, ideally with a screenshot or by highlighting it directly using your tool’s selection feature (if available).
    
- **Silent failures** are things that don’t work, but without any visible error. You click a button and nothing happens. Data that should appear is missing. A form submits but nothing changes. These are often the most frustrating issues because there’s no obvious signal to follow. The best approach is to describe the expected behavior vs. the actual behavior as precisely as you can, and if that doesn’t resolve it, ask the AI to add logging around the problem area (we’ll cover this shortly).
    
- **Explicit errors** are the most obvious—a blank screen, an error message on the page, or red text in the browser console—but they’re actually the easiest to fix. The error message tells you (or more importantly, tells the AI) exactly what went wrong. Copy the error, paste it into the chat, and let the AI diagnose it. Many tools make this possible with a single click. We’ll walk through this workflow in the next section.
    

## 

## Using the browser console

When something isn’t working—a button does nothing, data fails to load, the page goes blank—the browser console (a built-in panel in your browser that shows errors and log messages from the page) often tells you exactly what went wrong. It’s the most underused debugging tool among non-developers, yet it’s remarkably simple to use.

### 

## How to open the console

Press **F12** on Windows/Linux or **Cmd+Option+I** on Mac, then click the **Console** tab. Every modern browser supports this—Chrome, Firefox, Safari, Edge. That’s all it takes.

### 

## What to look for

Console messages are color-coded. **Red messages** are errors—something went wrong and needs attention. **Yellow messages** are warnings—non-critical issues that might cause unexpected behavior but probably aren’t your immediate problem. White or gray messages are informational and usually harmless.

When you see multiple red errors, **focus on the first one**. Later errors are often cascading consequences of the initial failure—fix the root cause, and the rest often disappear.

### 

## Reading error messages

Error messages in the console look intimidating, but you don’t need to understand them—the AI does. Your job is to copy the error and paste it into the chat. That said, it helps to know that every error message follows the same basic structure, so you can get a sense of what went wrong.

Here’s a typical error message:

TypeError: Cannot read properties of undefined (reading 'map')       
at CommentsSection.tsx:24:15

Every error has three parts: the **type** (TypeError—what kind of problem it is), the **description** (Cannot read properties of undefined—what went wrong in plain-ish English), and the **location** (CommentsSection.tsx:24—which file and line number).

### 

## The core workflow: copy, paste, fix

Once you spot a red error in the console, the debugging loop is simple:

1. Copy the error message
    
2. Paste it into the AI chat
    
3. Let the AI diagnose and fix the issue
    
4. Test to see if it worked
    
5. Repeat if necessary
    

The most effective prompt template for feeding errors to the AI: describe what you expected to happen, describe what actually happened, paste the exact error message, and mention any recent changes that might be relevant. For example:

“The comments section should display existing comments when I expand it, but it’s showing a blank area instead. Here’s the error from the browser console: TypeError: Cannot read properties of undefined (reading ‘map’) at CommentsSection.tsx:24. I recently changed the data structure for comments—could that be related?”

![](https://cdn5.dcbstatic.com/files/r/e/reforge_docebosaas_com/1790290800/UoviUdyE0A9VxxSvgzh7GQ/scorm/4c7a9d8af9f1c9ff6100277eff0737e7d11e6a117404ad4be1299da44cd1b846/scormcontent/assets/help_me_fix_2.jpg)

Most tools have streamlined this workflow. Reforge Build features a **“Help me fix”** button that appears when one or more errors show up in the Console errors pane. Lovable displays an error dialog with a **“Try to Fix”** button that scans logs and attempts an automatic patch—reportedly resolving about 60% of simple issues instantly.

## 

## Asking the AI to investigate

Sometimes the error message alone isn’t enough. The AI fixes the reported error, but the underlying problem persists—or the fix introduces a new issue. When you’re stuck in this pattern, it’s time to ask the AI to help you investigate more deeply.

### 

## Adding logging to trace the problem

You can ask the AI to add console.log statements—checkpoints that print out what the code is doing at each step. This is like asking someone to narrate their work out loud so you can hear where things go off track.

Effective prompts for this:

“The task list should show 5 items but it’s showing nothing. Add console.log statements so I can see whether the data is being loaded and what’s happening at each step before it reaches the screen.”

“When I submit a comment, nothing appears. Add console.log statements to show me what happens after I click the submit button (i.e., what data gets created and where it goes).”

After the AI adds the logging, reproduce the issue, then open the console. You’ll see a trail of messages showing what happened at each step. Copy those messages and paste them back into the AI—this gives the AI much more diagnostic information than the original error alone.

### 

## Using chat or discussion mode

Some tools offer a mode where you can discuss problems with the AI without it making code changes. Lovable calls this **Chat Mode** (as opposed to Edit mode). This is valuable when you want to analyze a problem before committing to a fix—especially when previous fix attempts have made things worse.

A useful prompt in this mode:

“Investigate to identify the root cause of the issue related to comment submission. Don’t make any changes yet—let’s understand the problem first. Report back on what you find and let me know if you have any questions.”

This prevents the AI from jumping to a quick patch and instead gets it to investigate the problem systematically before proposing a fix.

### 

## The three-strike rule

If the AI fails to fix an issue after two to three attempts, take a step back. Continuing to patch at this point usually makes things worse—each failed fix adds more noise to the conversation, making it harder for the AI to reason about the problem.

When you hit three strikes, you have several options:

1. Revert to a known good version
    
2. Start a fresh chat if your tool supports chat threads
    
3. Duplicate or fork your project to try a different approach
    
4. Or in some cases, rebuild from scratch
    

The next sections cover each of these techniques.

## 

## Managing versions

Version history is your safety net. Every major prototyping tool automatically saves a new version each time the AI makes changes, giving you a timeline of your project’s evolution. This means you can always go back to a state that was working.

### 

## How version history works

The core idea is the same across tools: each prompt you send creates a new version. You can browse previous versions, preview them, and restore one if the current state isn’t working. Every major prototyping tool—Reforge Build, Lovable, Bolt, v0, Replit—has version history either built into the product or available through GitHub integration, and they all work in similar ways.

Here’s what version history looks like in Reforge Build:

![](https://cdn5.dcbstatic.com/files/r/e/reforge_docebosaas_com/1790290800/UoviUdyE0A9VxxSvgzh7GQ/scorm/4c7a9d8af9f1c9ff6100277eff0737e7d11e6a117404ad4be1299da44cd1b846/scormcontent/assets/version_history.png)

The Version History panel shows a list of every change made to the prototype, each automatically named by the AI based on what was changed—“Fix React root ID mismatch,” “Add Kanban Retrospective Board,” “Update Action items Column Color.” You can scroll through the full history and click the revert button on any version to restore your project to that state. This makes it easy to find the last point where everything was working and roll back to it.

### 

## Reverting as a debugging technique

When the AI’s fixes aren’t working—especially after two or three attempts—revert to the last version where things were stable and try a different approach. This isn’t giving up. It’s the debugging equivalent of backing out of a wrong turn rather than driving further down a dead-end road.

After reverting, try your prompt again to see if it does better the second time around. Remember, AI is probabilistic—you may get a different (and better) result on a retry. If that doesn’t work, revert again and try a more specific or more robust version of your prompt. In particular, take notice of what went wrong in the previous attempts and add explicit instructions so the AI doesn’t repeat the same mistake.

### 

## Duplicating / forking your prototype

For bigger experiments—trying a fundamentally different approach to a feature, testing a risky change, or exploring an alternative design—duplicate your entire project first. This gives you a safe copy to fall back to if the experiment doesn’t work out.

Every major tool supports this. Reforge Build has a Duplicate feature that copies the current version of the prototype and summarizes all previous context so you can pick up where you left off. Lovable calls its version “Remix,” Bolt has “Duplicate,” and Replit has “Fork”. The key difference from version history is that a duplicate is a completely independent copy—you can take it in a radically different direction without any risk to your original.

### Saving snapshots of your prototype

It’s also good practice to duplicate your prototype at key milestones—after finishing a major feature, before attempting a risky change, or when you reach a stable state you want to preserve. This gives you a set of named snapshots you can return to if things go sideways. For complex prototypes where you’ve gone back and forth with the AI many times, version history can get long and hard to navigate. A small collection of milestone duplicates—“v1 basic layout,” “v2 with comments,” “v3 with styling polish”—is often easier to work with. Saving named snapshots at key phases of development is a common practice in software engineering, and it works just as well for prototyping.

### 

## GitHub as an external safety net

As your prototypes grow in complexity, GitHub provides a version control system that’s independent of any single tool. You don’t need GitHub to get started—many people build and ship entirely within their prototyping tool—but it becomes valuable as projects mature.

GitHub serves two purposes for prototypers. First, it’s an external backup. If anything goes wrong with your prototyping tool—or if you want to preserve a specific state of your project—GitHub has it stored independently. Second, it’s a bridge between tools. Since multiple prototyping tools can sync with GitHub, you can prototype in one tool and continue in another. This is especially useful when transitioning from a prototyping tool to a more powerful development environment.

As we saw in [The Vibe Coding Landscape(opens in a new tab)](https://edu.reforge.com/learn/courses/7165/ai-prototyping/lessons/14114:6754/introduction-to-prototyping), you can migrate your code to more powerful tools as your needs become more sophisticated. However, this is a one-way street—it’s often not possible to go back to a more high-level tool. For example, you can move your code from Lovable to Claude Code, but you can’t move from Claude Code back to Lovable.

Many tools—including Lovable, Bolt, v0, and Replit—offer built-in GitHub integration that automatically syncs your code to a repository. For tools that don’t support direct GitHub integration, like Reforge Build, you can download your code and add it to a GitHub repository manually to maintain a backup.

The practical value is straightforward: GitHub gives you an undo button that lives outside your prototyping tool, and it lets you move your code between tools without starting over.

## 

## Managing context as your prototype grows

As your prototyping sessions get longer, the amount of context grows. Although the latest LLMs have very large “context windows” (meaning they can technically handle a lot of context), the performance of the LLM starts to suffer long before the context window runs out. You may notice issues like the AI forgetting decisions made earlier in the conversation, inadvertently reverting features to prior versions, using inconsistent styling, or generating buggy code.

This is known **context rot**—and it’s one of the most common frustrations in longer prototyping sessions.

![](https://cdn5.dcbstatic.com/files/r/e/reforge_docebosaas_com/1790290800/UoviUdyE0A9VxxSvgzh7GQ/scorm/4c7a9d8af9f1c9ff6100277eff0737e7d11e6a117404ad4be1299da44cd1b846/scormcontent/assets/AI%20Product%20Management..jpg)

Context rot occurs when an LLM gets overwhelmed by irrelevant data in its context window. The graph above shows how an LLM’s ability to retrieve relevant content degrades as the size of the context window grows.

The first step in addressing this is to actively manage context. That means using the “minimal viable” context necessary to get desired results. As you’ve seen, we’re using terse prompts which have the relevant info but no fluff. These prompts are specific enough to be effective without providing irrelevant and distracting details.

![](https://cdn5.dcbstatic.com/files/r/e/reforge_docebosaas_com/1790290800/UoviUdyE0A9VxxSvgzh7GQ/scorm/4c7a9d8af9f1c9ff6100277eff0737e7d11e6a117404ad4be1299da44cd1b846/scormcontent/assets/3_M2L4/AI%20Product%20Management..jpg)

However, prototyping is particularly taxing on context. Even when you're as efficient as possible, long sessions will start to overwhelm the LLM. A handful of practical techniques help keep this in check.

**Start from a template.** A well-structured starter project gives the AI immediate context about your code patterns, architecture, and conventions—dramatically more effective than starting blank and explaining everything through chat.

**Break complex work into multiple prompts.** Divide work into focused steps instead of one massive prompt. This keeps the LLM's recent working memory relevant and produces better outputs.

**Use your prototyping tool's context features.** Many tools support project-level files—like [CLAUDE.md(opens in a new tab)](http://claude.md/) for Claude Code or the Project context for Reforge Build. Put your design system, conventions, and architecture decisions in these files so you don't repeat yourself every session. Think of it as documentation that the AI actually reads.

**Write context into files, not just chat.** Your prototyping tool writes code into files, but you can also ask it to write other files—a JSON file for mock data, a ReadMe for critical project information. Once that information is written to a file, the LLM doesn't need to keep it in its short-term working memory, i.e., its context. It can read that file as needed in the future.

**Compact at natural breakpoints.** Many tools can summarize your conversation history, preserving key decisions without carrying forward every message. This happens automatically, but you can trigger it manually by duplicating or forking your project. Some tools also have explicit compaction features. For example, you can use the /compact command in Claude Code to summarize the context.

**Use multiple tools as a pipeline.** For example, use Claude to generate a JSON file with mock data, then attach that file into your prototyping tool. This approach works like building your own multi-agent system—each tool focuses on what it does best and works with only the most relevant context.

The underlying principle: **context is a resource you actively manage**, not something you set once and forget.

## 

## When to start over

The most counterintuitive debugging technique is also one of the most effective: rebuild from scratch.

Starting over feels drastic, but it’s actually one of the unique advantages of AI prototyping. In traditional software development, rebuilding from scratch is enormously expensive. With AI tools, it is a viable strategy because the cost of building is so low.

Sometimes, building a new prototype is faster than untangling a codebase that’s been patched too many times. There are telltale signs to look for:

- You’re stuck in a loop: fixes keep creating new problems.
    
- The AI seems confused by the codebase, not just the current task.
    
- Multiple features are interacting in ways that produce cascading bugs.
    
- You’re spending more time debugging than building.
    

When you’re hitting these walls, starting from scratch can be the fastest path forward—because you now know what works, what to ask for, and what to avoid. It takes less time the second time. The rebuild will be faster, cleaner, and more stable.

### 

## How to rebuild effectively

Because starting over is always an option, it’s worth saving your prompts as you go. A well-written prompt is a reusable blueprint—you can replay it in a new project to regenerate the same feature, often with better results the second time around.

Start a new project and replay the prompts you’ve saved, making adjustments based on what you’ve learned. Build incrementally: get one feature working and stable before adding the next.

A useful addition to your prompts: “Ask me clarifying questions before implementing this.” This catches misunderstandings early and gives you implementation options before any code is written.

**Consider simplifying features that were causing problems.** If the AI struggled with a particular API integration, for example, you can create a mock version that removes the dependency and gets you to a working prototype without it. From there, you can figure out the real integration separately—or you may decide you don’t need to. Your prototype might already answer the question you set out to test, and the thornier implementation work can wait for the production version.

## 

## When to graduate to a more powerful tool

Sometimes the right debugging move isn’t another prompt—it’s a tool change.

As your prototype grows, you may run into issues that are hard (or impossible) to solve inside your current environment. This is the same “graduation” idea we covered in [The Vibe Coding Landscape(opens in a new tab)](https://edu.reforge.com/learn/courses/7165/ai-prototyping/lessons/14114:6754/introduction-to-prototyping): start with the simplest tool that gets you moving, but be willing to move to something more powerful when the work demands it.

### 

## First, ask: Is this complexity essential?

Before you invest in a more heavyweight build, pause and ask whether you actually need the complicated functionality.

- **What decision is the prototype meant to support?**
    
- **Is this feature essential to answer that question, or is it “nice to have”?**
    
- **Can you simulate the behavior** with mock data, hard-coded states, or a simplified flow?
    

One of the easiest traps in prototyping is confusing “making the prototype perfect” with “getting to clarity.” The goal is to learn and decide, not to build production software.

### 

## Signals that it’s time to graduate

Consider moving to a more powerful tool (or bringing in engineering help) when:

- **The functionality is genuinely complicated.** For example, multi-step workflows with lots of edge cases, real-time collaboration, complex permissions, or performance-sensitive behavior.
    
- **You need deeper control** over architecture, dependencies, or project structure than your current tool supports.
    
- **You need a real integration** with production systems (APIs, auth, data models) and the prototype has moved beyond “fake it” territory.
    
- **You’re stuck debugging the same class of issues** and you’ve exhausted the ladder: you keep patching, but the system stays unstable.
    

## What “graduating” can look like

Graduating does not have to mean rewriting everything immediately. Common paths:

- **Move the code into a more powerful AI development tool** (like Cursor or Claude Code) to get better debugging, structure, and control.
    
- **Hand off (or pair) with an engineer** on your team when the prototype’s learning goal requires more sophisticated implementation.
    
- **Use the prototype as a spec.** Sometimes the highest leverage move is to stop implementing and use the prototype to make requirements unambiguous for production development.
    

If the extra sophistication is necessary to answer the core question your prototype is trying to resolve, graduating can be the fastest path to a stable build that lets you make the decision and move on.

## 

## Putting it all together

Debugging is a conversation with the AI. Your job is to be a good collaborator—describe problems precisely, provide evidence (screenshots, console errors, logs), and know when to change tactics instead of pushing harder on the same approach.

Most issues resolve with simple techniques: reloading, describing the problem clearly, copying a console error into the chat. When they don’t, you have a systematic ladder to climb—from adding logging, to reverting, to duplicating, to starting fresh.

The habits that separate productive prototypers from those who spend hours spinning in circles come down to three things: describe problems specifically, save your work before risky changes, and don’t be afraid to start over. The speed of AI prototyping is your safety net—it makes every debugging technique, even the most drastic ones, faster than you’d expect.
