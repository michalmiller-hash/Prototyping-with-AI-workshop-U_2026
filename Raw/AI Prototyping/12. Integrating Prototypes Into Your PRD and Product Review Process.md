Prototypes are the most powerful alignment tool a product team has—but only if they're woven into your existing workflows. In this lesson, you'll learn how to embed prototypes into your PRDs as living references that replace static screenshots and abstract descriptions, and how to use them in product reviews to drive faster, higher-quality decisions. We'll cover how to structure a prototype-driven PRD, when to present prototypes versus share them asynchronously, and how to use prototypes to surface disagreements and open questions early—before engineering commits resources.

Tom and David Kelley, co-founders of the renowned design and innovation consultancy IDEO, have a saying:

_"If a picture is worth a thousand words,_ **_a prototype is worth a thousand meetings_**_."_

Product teams have known this for decades. The value of prototyping has never been in question. What's been in question is whether most teams could afford to do it.

As we discussed in [The New Product Development Lifecycle(opens in a new tab)](https://edu.reforge.com/learn/courses/7165/ai-prototyping/lessons/14114:6754/introduction-to-prototyping), the entire product development lifecycle has—until recently—been shaped by one constraint: building working software is expensive. The process started with a spec or PRD, not because it was the best place to start, but because it was the cheapest. The PRD was the least expensive artifact a team could create to begin the process of discovery, alignment, and conviction-building that would justify further investment.

Today, that constraint is dissolving. As we've seen throughout this course, it's now possible to create powerful prototypes quickly and easily — sometimes faster than you can write a spec. Madhu Guru, Head of Product for Google Gemini, captured this shift in [a post(opens in a new tab)](https://x.com/realmadhuguru/status/1950336434126336154) that stirred significant conversation across the product community:

![](https://cdn5.dcbstatic.com/files/r/e/reforge_docebosaas_com/1790290800/UoviUdyE0A9VxxSvgzh7GQ/scorm/27a4bb54c2353d3917c607717782e2eea18a1559a925dfe2a934ec528bccbdbb/scormcontent/assets/Screenshot%202026-03-16%20.png)

This is changing the product development lifecycle in fundamental ways. The traditional PRD — thick enough to be a doorstop and stuffed with details nobody reads — is dying. Prototypes communicate what words cannot.

But the PRD itself? More important than ever. When you can prototype in a weekend, the bottleneck isn't describing what to build — it's knowing _why_ you're building it.

Prototypes enable us to move faster. PRDs make sure that speed is aimed in the right direction.

The strongest product teams are shifting to a **prototype-centered workflow**:

- The **PRD becomes lighter and more decision-oriented** — focused on strategy, not specification.
    
- The **prototype becomes the reference** for behavior, flows, and tradeoffs.
    
- **Product reviews** focus less on reading specs and more on making decisions, testing assumptions, and answering open questions.
    

In this lesson, we'll explore how to make that shift — how to write PRDs that work hand-in-hand with prototypes, how to run product reviews that turn prototypes into decision-making machines, and how to adapt the process to your team's specific needs.

## Prototypes Answer "What?", PRDs Answer "Why?"

Every company has its own version of the product development lifecycle. Different names, different stages, different rituals. But they all fundamentally answer the same five questions:

1. **What** do we need to build for customers?
    
2. **Why** are we building that thing?
    
3. **How** will we get it done?
    
4. **When** will we launch it?
    
5. **Where** will customers find it?
    

![](https://cdn5.dcbstatic.com/files/r/e/reforge_docebosaas_com/1790290800/UoviUdyE0A9VxxSvgzh7GQ/scorm/27a4bb54c2353d3917c607717782e2eea18a1559a925dfe2a934ec528bccbdbb/scormcontent/assets/five-questions.svg)

AI prototyping gives us a radically better answer to the first question. Instead of describing what we want to build in words — words that ten people will read ten different ways — we can put a working version in front of ourselves, our stakeholders, and our customers. There is no better way to answer "what."

Figma PM Holly Li's team saw this firsthand. They were debating scrolling behavior for a feature — the kind of nuanced interaction where everyone has a strong opinion, but nobody can quite put it into words. In Figma's article ["Prototypes Are the New PRDs,"(opens in a new tab)](https://www.figma.com/blog/prototypes-are-the-new-prds/) Li described the specifics of speed, direction, and edge behavior as "an almost impossible thing to describe" in a document. So instead of arguing over language, her team built prototypes of each approach and let everyone experience them directly. The debate ended quickly.

That's the power of showing over telling. It answers the “What?” far more clearly than a spec. But it does not answer other critical questions, most importantly, “Why?”

## The thinking that building can't replace

Madhu Guru's post about Google's shift from writing to building stirred an important response — because the thinking that writing enables is not perfectly replaced by building. Building helps us explore, sharpen, and communicate our ideas. But it leaves important strategic thinking undone.

Here's what a prototype doesn't tell you:

- A prototype can't justify the **strategic bet** — the rationale for building this instead of something else.
    
- It can't explain **timing** — the market conditions or internal factors that make this the right moment.
    
- It doesn't define **success criteria** — the metrics and outcomes that will tell you whether it worked.
    
- It doesn't surface **opportunity cost** — the tradeoffs you're making by pursuing this direction.
    
- It says nothing about **distribution** — the plan for getting this in front of customers once it's built.
    
- And it can't articulate **scope boundaries** — what you're deliberately leaving out, and why.
    

Sachin Rekhi [put it concisely(opens in a new tab)](https://x.com/sachinrekhi/status/1968329141780177022): 1) prototypes don't speak to product strategy, 2) they rarely include all required functionality (settings pages and ancillary experiences are still faster to describe in words), and 3) they can't communicate non-UI requirements — some of the most crucial aspects of our products are things users never see.

OpenAI reinforced this perspective. Sean Grove, in [his widely-shared talk "The New Code,"(opens in a new tab)](https://www.news.aakashg.com/p/ai-prd) described how OpenAI actively regards specs as one of their most important documents — because specs force the kind of strategic thinking that prototypes can't.

## Two artifacts, one system

PRDs and prototypes solve fundamentally different problems. A PRD explains _why_ you're building something, how you'll measure success, and what constraints you're operating within. A prototype shows _what_ it looks and feels like. Together, they're far more powerful than either one alone.

The old model was to write a long PRD, attach some mockups, review everything in meetings, and then hand off to engineering — with a lot of key decisions left either open or entirely undiscovered.

The emerging model: define the problem and constraints in a lightweight PRD, embed or link an interactive prototype, use the prototype in reviews to expose disagreements quickly, then iterate on both artifacts as the team builds the confidence necessary to launch.

In the old model, the PRD tried to be everything. In the new model, prototypes and PRDs each do what they do best.

## Which Comes First — PRD or Prototype?

The short answer: it depends. And that's the point.

## From assembly line to jazz band

![](https://cdn5.dcbstatic.com/files/r/e/reforge_docebosaas_com/1790290800/UoviUdyE0A9VxxSvgzh7GQ/scorm/27a4bb54c2353d3917c607717782e2eea18a1559a925dfe2a934ec528bccbdbb/scormcontent/assets/image%20%281%29.jpg)

As we talked about in [The New Product Development Lifecycle(opens in a new tab)](https://edu.reforge.com/learn/courses/7165/ai-prototyping/lessons/14114:6754/introduction-to-prototyping), we're moving away from the risk-gated, linear assembly line that carefully traded signal for risk as teams marched toward having enough conviction to build production software. We're moving toward what I described as a jazz band — where working software is part of every stage of the process, and teams work together collaboratively and iteratively rather than passing artifacts down a sequential chain.

This means prototypes don't always follow PRDs. Sometimes, a prototype _is_ how you figure out what the PRD should say. Sometimes the PRD comes first, and the prototype pressure-tests whether the strategy translates into a compelling experience. Often, the two evolve together — you start with a rough PRD, use it to define an initial prototype, get feedback, adjust the PRD, build more prototypes, and along the way accumulate signal and conviction.

## The four prototype types as a guide

![](https://cdn5.dcbstatic.com/files/r/e/reforge_docebosaas_com/1790290800/UoviUdyE0A9VxxSvgzh7GQ/scorm/27a4bb54c2353d3917c607717782e2eea18a1559a925dfe2a934ec528bccbdbb/scormcontent/assets/prototype-prd-timing.svg)

This brings us back to a central theme of this course: prototypes are decision-making tools. Each type helps you at a different point in the process, and each has a different relationship to the PRD:

- **Concept prototypes** often come _before_ a PRD. They help you map the solution space and figure out which direction to take. You might build three rough concepts before you even know what to write in a spec.
    
- **Design prototypes** are often companions _within_ a PRD. They take a chosen concept and add enough detail to lock in a direction and align the team. The PRD provides the strategic framing; the prototype shows the experience.
    
- **Research prototypes** generate insights that _inform_ a PRD. Customer reactions to a research prototype help you refine not just the "what," but the "why," "when," and "where" of your strategy.
    
- **Technical prototypes** often come _after_ a PRD, when the team needs to test the boundary between what they aspire to create and what's technically achievable.
    

This isn't ironclad. A concept prototype might come after a first-draft PRD if the team already has a strong hypothesis. A technical prototype might come first if feasibility is the highest-risk question. The mapping above is a useful default, not a rigid rule.

## The real question

The important question is not "which comes first?" It's:

**What's the important decision we need to make right now, and how will the combination of a well-written PRD and a well-built prototype help us make it?**

Sometimes the path from discovery to delivery is very short — a lightweight PRD, a single prototype, one round of review, and you're building. Sometimes it requires extensive iteration across multiple prototypes as the team converges on the right solution for a particularly tricky problem.

The fast pace of prototyping also means we can throw out a lot more work. Dead ends that used to cost weeks now cost hours. This kind of exploratory iteration used to be the privileged domain of large companies with big research budgets. Anthropic [reportedly cycles through 10 or more prototypes(opens in a new tab)](https://newsletter.pragmaticengineer.com/p/how-claude-code-is-built) for a single feature, each iteration compressed to hours by AI coding tools. That kind of rapid experimentation is now accessible to anyone.

## What a Prototype-Driven PRD Looks Like

When prototypes carry the burden of showing what you're building, the PRD is free to do what it was always meant to do: provide strategic clarity.

## The PRD shrinks — and sharpens

In the past, a significant share of every PRD was dedicated to describing the "what." Intricate software interactions required lengthy written descriptions — and despite their length, those descriptions quickly got out of sync with what was actually being built. Even before AI, most PMs could relate to the experience of writing a detailed spec that nobody fully read.

Prototypes remove that burden. Now the PRD can focus on strategy, constraints, and decisions — the things a prototype can't show you. The template below maps this out in detail, but the throughline is simple: the PRD owns the reasoning, the prototype owns the experience.

One element deserves special emphasis: non-goals. Saying what you're _not_ going to do often provides a sharper definition of what you _are_ going to do. In a world where prototypes make it easy to build, the discipline of deciding what _not_ to build becomes even more critical.

## The prototype-driven PRD template

Here's a structure that works well when prototypes are a first-class artifact. Notice how the first three sections are entirely about strategy and context — the prototype doesn't appear until you get to the solution.

|Section|What it covers|Prototype's role|
|---|---|---|
|**Problem statement**|Who has this problem, and why it matters|n/a|
|**Hypothesis & strategy fit**|Why we believe this is the right strategic bet|n/a|
|**Goals & success metrics**|What success looks like, how we measure it|n/a|
|**Solution overview**|How are we solving this problem?|Share a prototype **with context**|
|**Key interactions & flows**|Detailed walkthrough of critical paths|Share a video walkthrough of the prototype|
|**Open questions**|Unresolved decisions that need input|If applicable, share prototype variations that explore options|
|**Scope & non-goals**|What we're not building and why|Call out how these decisions show up in the prototype|
|**Risks and assumptions**|Key assumptions that must hold for this to succeed, and the biggest risks to getting there|Note which assumptions the prototype can validate and which need other evidence|
|**Technical considerations**|Architecture, dependencies, and constraints that shape the solution|Reference _prototype code_ where it illustrates the technical approach|
|**Rollout & go-to-market**|Launch plan and phasing|n/a|

## Your prototype can't always speak for itself

A prototype link without context is an invitation for unfocused feedback. You'll get comments on font sizes when you need input on the information architecture. Remember the "Share a prototype **with context**" note in the PRD template above — that context is doing real work. Every time you share a prototype — whether it's embedded in a PRD, dropped into Slack, or presented in a review — include:

- The **prototype link** itself
    
- The **user scenario** it demonstrates
    
- **What to focus on** — the specific flows, interactions, or decisions you want people to react to
    
- **What's not in scope** — the things you deliberately left out or didn't polish
    
- **Open questions** — the decisions you haven't made yet and need input on
    
- **What specific feedback are you looking for** — so people know what kind of response is actually useful
    

This framing matters because of a fundamental truth about prototypes: _a prototype is not production software_. You have to draw lines — what to include and what to leave out, where to invest and where to cut corners. Those lines are the whole point. They're what make a prototype a useful decision-making tool rather than an unfinished product.

But the people reviewing your prototype don't know where you drew those lines unless you tell them. They don't know which interactions reflect deliberate product decisions and which ones the AI just happened to generate. They can't tell whether the three-step onboarding flow was a carefully considered choice or an approximation of a more detailed experience.

So be explicit about boundaries. Call out which parts represent real product decisions — the flows you thought through, the interactions you designed, the scope you intentionally chose. And call out which parts are just scaffolding — the color palette the AI picked, the placeholder copy, the edge cases you haven't thought through yet. Without this clarity, your review might get derailed by a 20-minute debate about a shade of blue when the real question is whether the overall approach is right.

## How Prototypes Turn Product Reviews Into Decision Machines

The traditional product review was a high-stakes affair. When building working software was very expensive, the review served as a "speak now or forever hold your peace" moment. The real question behind every review was: do we have enough conviction to make the giant leap and invest in building this?

The ritual varied from company to company, but the dynamics were remarkably similar: a pot-committed team that didn't want to lose weeks or months of hard work by going back to the drawing board, skeptical leadership deciding what gets resources, and sheepish stakeholders offering feedback tempered by the knowledge it probably wouldn't be heard. A ritual meant to be defined by clarity and candor often turned into a dog-and-pony show.

AI prototyping allows us to reshape the product review into what it was always meant to be: an opportunity to leverage experience and perspectives from across the company to make our products better.

## Everyone nods, but nobody sees the same thing

In [Context Engineering for Prototyping,(opens in a new tab)](https://edu.reforge.com/learn/courses/7165/ai-prototyping/lessons/14115:6755/prototyping-fundamentals) we talked about a simple thought experiment: would you ever build a house without blueprints? Of course not — an architect could give you the most detailed written spec in the world, but the plan isn't complete until you can see it. The same is true for product reviews. When the only shared artifact is a written document, everyone reads the same words but pictures something different.

In ["How to Implement AI Prototyping In Your Product Development Cycle,"(opens in a new tab)](https://www.reforge.com/blog/ai-prototyping-product-development) a PM told Reforge: "I can sit there and write out each field that should be on a form and then have the engineer misinterpret that. Or we can both look at the same HTML."

We've all been in the same boat. At a certain point, PRDs hit diminishing returns on clarity. No amount of extra detail can resolve the misalignment that inevitably happens when people try to imagine software from a written description.

When stakeholders interact with working software, the conversation shifts. "I guess that might work" turns into "I just tried it — here's what I noticed." The quality of feedback improves dramatically because it's **grounded in experience rather than imagination**.

## Surfacing disagreements before engineering commits

Perhaps the most valuable function of prototypes in the product review process is surfacing hidden disagreements and revealing edge cases before a single line of production code is written.

The economics are stark. Errors caught during prototyping cost a fraction of the errors discovered in production. Written specs create an illusion of alignment. Prototypes shatter that illusion — revealing critical issues that can be solved while the cost of change is still low.

The clarity provided by prototypes benefits the whole organization. Designers can spot confusing interactions. Engineers can better assess technical feasibility. Customer support can anticipate issues. Marketing teams can figure out messaging. PMs can refine the scope based on concrete reactions rather than speculation.

As [Figma's product team observed(opens in a new tab)](https://www.figma.com/blog/prototypes-are-the-new-prds/): product decisions often hinge on details that are impossible to imagine until you can actually interact with them. How fast should a loading state appear? Does a layout make sense when you click through it?

The shift is significant: the expensive surprises that used to surface during engineering — when changes require refactoring code — now surface during prototyping, when changes require only editing prompts.

## Async sharing vs. live demos: when each works best

The choice between sharing prototypes asynchronously and presenting them live depends on the decision context. A useful rule of thumb: **async works well for discussion; live works well for debate.**

||**Async sharing**|**Live demo**|
|---|---|---|
|**Best for…**|Discussion|Debate|
|**Use when…**|Stakeholders need time to explore independently, teams span time zones, or it'll take days to align calendars|Decisions require real-time debate, complex interactions need a narrative walkthrough, or you're comparing multiple options|
|**You provide…**|The packaging — the prototype and its context need to stand on their own|The narrative — you guide attention and respond to reactions in the moment|
|**Feedback style**|Written, asynchronous, better for documentation and thoughtful responses|Verbal, real-time, often leads to decisions on the spot|
|**Come prepared with…**|The sharing checklist from above (prototype link, user scenario, focus areas, scope, open questions, feedback prompt), plus a deadline for comments|The specific decision to be made, a planned demo path, open questions, and a named decision owner|
|**Add if needed**|A Loom walkthrough for complex flows that aren't self-explanatory|A short framing doc sent in advance so attendees arrive with context|

Whichever format you choose, your prototype can't always speak for itself. Use the sharing checklist from earlier in this lesson — it applies to both live and async reviews.

## Frame every review around a decision

Whether live or async, a prototype review should not be a generic walkthrough. Every review should answer six questions:

1. **What decision do we need to make?**
    
2. **What does the prototype prove?**
    
3. **What does it _not_ prove?**
    
4. **What are the riskiest assumptions?**
    
5. **What disagreements need to be resolved?**
    
6. **What still needs validation before we move into production?**
    

This framing prevents the most common failure mode of prototype reviews — getting unfocused feedback on visual details when you need strategic alignment.

## Speed of Learning Is the New Competitive Advantage

Speed in product development is often more about latency than velocity. Velocity measures how much work you're getting done. Latency measures how quickly you can go from a hypothesis to an insight. They're very different things.

The fastest teams don't necessarily ship the most code. They have an incredibly fast learning loop that compounds. They identify a hypothesis, test it, learn from it, and feed those learnings back into their next decision.

Teams that move slowly often have lag that creeps in unseen. You can't get on the exec's calendar until next month. The next product review is two weeks away. Engineering can't start until quarterly planning wraps. Each delay seems minor, but they accumulate. A team that ships once a month may feel like it's moving fast — but that team only gets twelve at-bats per year. Twelve opportunities to learn and feed that learning back into the process.

AI prototyping compresses the build-measure-learn loop from weeks to hours — and sometimes to minutes.

I work with a team that prototypes in real time during customer interviews. They'll start a 45-to-60-minute session by understanding the customer's preferences and pain points — then build a working prototype of the feature during the conversation. By the end of the session, they walk the customer through a prototype designed around that customer's needs. In a single meeting, they've not only shaped the problem; they've validated the solution. That's the kind of speed that turns into a competitive advantage.

We can already see that pace is being set by the most AI-native companies. Those companies are responding to market needs in weeks, not quarters.

## Match the process to the problem

Not every initiative needs an extensive discovery process. For a well-understood problem with a small team and moderate stakes, the whole cycle can compress to a few steps: a lightweight PRD, a single prototype, one round of review, and you're building.

But when the problem is ambiguous, the stakes are high, or multiple stakeholders need alignment, you need a more robust process:

1. **Explore** — Build two or three concept prototypes to map the solution space. No PRD yet.
    
2. **Frame** — Write a first-draft PRD that captures your emerging hypothesis, strategic rationale, and success metrics.
    
3. **Align** — Build a design prototype with enough detail to lock in the approach. Run a live review. Update the PRD with decisions made.
    
4. **Validate** — Put a research prototype in front of customers. (We'll cover this in  Testing Prototypes With Customers next.) Feed what you learn back into the PRD.
    
5. **De-risk** — If there are open technical questions, build a technical prototype to test feasibility.
    
6. **Commit** — Hand off both artifacts — the refined PRD and the latest prototype — to engineering. We’ll discuss that in Handing Prototypes Off to Design & Engineering. 
    

The PRD and prototype evolve together through each phase. The PRD gets sharper as prototypes surface new information. The prototypes get more focused as the PRD clarifies what matters. What changes between the quick path and the thorough path is the number of loops, not the logic.

## Failure is now affordable

The fast pace of prototyping means you can afford to throw out more work. Dead ends that used to cost weeks/months now cost hours. That kind of exploratory experimentation used to be the privileged domain of large companies with large product development and research teams. Those large companies could afford to fail.

Now, the cost of failure is much lower. We don’t need to stick to the plan because of sunk costs. As a better path emerges, we can take it. The wrong turns and dead ends become exactly the thing that we need to get to the right destination.

## The Spec Is Dead. Long Live the Spec.

The traditional PRD is dying, but a new and much more effective approach is emerging.

The most effective teams maintain both artifacts: a **lean strategic document** covering hypothesis, strategy fit, success metrics, non-goals, risks, and rollout plan, paired with an **interactive prototype** that demonstrates the experience and serves as the primary communication tool across product, design, engineering, execs, other stakeholders, and customers.

**Prototypes are the fastest way to create shared understanding.** They replace the ambiguity of written descriptions with something everyone can see, touch, and react to. Use them throughout the product development process — not just at the end.

**PRDs still matter for strategy, constraints, and decisions.** The thinking that writing forces — defining hypotheses, setting metrics, articulating non-goals, planning rollouts — is not replaced by building. If anything, it becomes more important when the cost of building drops, because the risk shifts from "can we build it?" to "should we build it?"

**The real differentiator is the team rituals that connect the two.** The companies at the frontier — Anthropic, Figma, Linear — aren't just adding prototypes to their existing process. They're fundamentally restructuring how decisions flow through their organizations, moving from scheduled review gates to continuous feedback loops.

The competitive advantage isn't speed of prototyping. It's the speed of _learning_. Use AI-generated prototypes not to skip the thinking, but to think faster — making your ideas tangible enough to test, critique, and improve before committing engineering resources.
