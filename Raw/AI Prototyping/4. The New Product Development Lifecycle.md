**The old product lifecycle was designed around a fundamental constraint: building software is expensive. So we had to earn the right to build through cheaper artifacts—specs, wireframes, designs—each helping us build conviction before committing resources. AI removes that constraint and inverts the logic: we used to build conviction so we could build software; now we build software to build conviction. In this lesson, you'll learn when to use working software at each stage—exploration, alignment, validation—and how to build a team culture around this shift.**

## **Why is AI-powered prototyping a must-learn skill?**

## The constraint that shaped everything

How many customers are looking for a product that _just barely_ solves their need? Yet this is the philosophy that underpins "minimum viable product"—one of our most prevalent best practices.

Many of the best practices we take for granted weren't designed to help you build the best product. They were designed to avoid expensive mistakes.

MVPs don't make sense for the customer, but they make sense for us. We want to build the least amount possible to validate demand before investing further.

That's the foundational constraint of yesterday's product lifecycle: building software is expensive. So we avoid building it for as long as possible. And when we do build, we build as little as we can get away with.

During a typical product development cycle, we create a variety of artifacts—each requiring more effort but providing stronger signal:

![](https://cdn5.dcbstatic.com/files/r/e/reforge_docebosaas_com/1788184800/R30jyYFItmP8aqsz8_LeIQ/scorm/13c7a59b80838f515b3334984e5746b093c1baa4848e58c6b40e042f838020b2/scormcontent/assets/AI%20Builder%27s%20Firesid.jpg)

We start with specs—relatively quick to write and easy to iterate.

After specs, we move to visual designs. Wireframes come first—quick sketches to establish structure. Then detailed designs, sometimes pixel-perfect.

But here's the problem: even the most detailed designs can't capture how modern software actually feels. Animations, transitions, generative AI output, native platform interactions—these only make sense when you experience them. A Figma mockup can show you a screen. It can't show you whether an interaction feels right.

Still, we create these artifacts because we need conviction before we commit engineering resources. Visual designs are expensive to create and hard to iterate, but they're cheaper than building the wrong thing.

Then comes working software—and effort takes a jump. Teams often skip functional prototypes because they're nearly as difficult to build as an MVP. Finally, once the MVP has been validated, the team can move on to the expensive task of launching a full-scope product.

At each step, we get valuable signal that gives us the conviction to invest in the next step. If we don't get that conviction, we go back to square one—iterating cheaply until we figure out what's missing.

**This process wasn't designed to help you build great products. It was designed to manage the cost of building the wrong ones.**

## From assembly line to jazz band

For decades, that was the right approach. When building software is expensive, you manage costs carefully. Even the most agile teams operated as assembly lines—handoffs from one team to another, conviction required at every stage to justify the next.

![](https://cdn5.dcbstatic.com/files/r/e/reforge_docebosaas_com/1788184800/R30jyYFItmP8aqsz8_LeIQ/scorm/13c7a59b80838f515b3334984e5746b093c1baa4848e58c6b40e042f838020b2/scormcontent/assets/image%20%2818%29.jpg)

But what happens when the cost of building software drops dramatically?

**Today, AI is changing the fundamental constraint of product management.**

What once took weeks, now takes hours. As we saw in the first lesson, you can often create a working prototype in less time than it takes to write a detailed spec or sketch out a wireframe.

![](https://cdn5.dcbstatic.com/files/r/e/reforge_docebosaas_com/1788184800/R30jyYFItmP8aqsz8_LeIQ/scorm/13c7a59b80838f515b3334984e5746b093c1baa4848e58c6b40e042f838020b2/scormcontent/assets/K0x--P/AI%20Builder%27s%20Firesid.jpg)

When working software becomes cheap to produce, the assembly line stops making sense. Why wait until the end of the process to see your idea come to life? Why hand off a spec when you can provide a prototype that better reflects the requirements?

Moving forward, the best product teams will operate like a jazz band. Instead of staged handoffs, team members riff off each other—each leveraging their unique strengths to get the right artifacts in front of customers. The flow isn't one-directional. It loops back. It branches. Prototypes inform research. Research reshapes prototypes. A spec might be the first step, or it might be one of the last.

Working software is no longer just what we deliver at the end of the process. It’s integral to how we communicate, decide, and validate along the way.

## The new operating model

This isn't incremental—it's a fundamental shift in how we operate. In this new model:

1. **Things are moving faster.** We can build faster—but so can our competitors. The speed advantage isn't just ours. We're operating in an environment where the market landscape and customer expectations are changing as quickly as we can build.
    
2. **More people can build.** Prototyping used to require specialized design and development skills. Now PMs, founders, salespeople, customer success teams—anyone with product input—can turn hunches into working software. That's a flood of new possibilities, often from people closer to the customer. But it also means we need sharper prioritization and better validation to filter for what actually serves the customer.
    
3. **Exploration is now cheap.** You can build five solutions instead of committing to one. Hunches that would never justify the resource investment can now get tested. The math has flipped: what used to look like waste—building something you might throw away—is now the fastest path to the right answer.

### With AI prototyping, you can integrate working software into every phase of product discovery & development.

This is why prototyping has become a must-learn skill. Not because it's new and shiny—because it's now foundational.

**The best product builders are integrating working software into every phase of discovery and development.**

They prototype to explore. They prototype to align. They prototype to validate. Fluency with prototyping is now as fundamental as the ability to write a clear spec or run a good customer interview.

## Prototypes are decision-making tools

Prototyping is fun—and it's easy to get pulled into crafting the perfect thing. It's critical to remember that prototypes aren't the deliverable; they're a tool for making better and faster decisions.

This means the first question isn't "What should we prototype?" Instead, start by asking: "What decision are we trying to make?"

Your answer determines what you build:

- **Concept prototype** — _Exploring which direction to pursue?_ Build rough variations to map the solution space.
    
- **Design prototype** — _Aligning stakeholders on what we're building?_ Build something higher fidelity that gets everyone on the same page.
    
- **Research prototype** — _Validating whether customers will use it?_ Build something they can understand and get value from.
    
- **Technical prototype** — _Proving technical feasibility?_ Build something that tests how things work in the real world.
    

Let's walk through each type and when to reach for it.

![](https://cdn5.dcbstatic.com/files/r/e/reforge_docebosaas_com/1788184800/R30jyYFItmP8aqsz8_LeIQ/scorm/13c7a59b80838f515b3334984e5746b093c1baa4848e58c6b40e042f838020b2/scormcontent/assets/image%20%2820%29.jpg)

## Concept prototypes — Exploration

**When to use:** The problem is clear, but the solution isn't.

This is where AI prototyping shines. You've validated the problem space, but the team has competing ideas on how to solve it.

In the past, exploring all those ideas was impractical. Teams went with their gut, picked one direction, and ran with it—only to discover weeks or months later they'd chosen wrong.

Now you can build working concept prototypes for multiple approaches. Diverge before you converge. Test three directions instead of debating which one might be best.

At this stage, fidelity should be low—think initial sketch, not polished design. Use static data, mock APIs, and shortcuts that free up time to explore variations.

The risk used to be wasted resources. Now the risk is committing to a mediocre solution because you never explored the alternatives.

## Design prototypes — Definition & alignment

**When to use:** You've chosen a direction. Now you need to define the details—and get everyone on the same page.

This is where prototypes replace documents and decks.

One PM called design prototypes "the front end of the pitch." The PRD, JIRA tickets, and cost estimates still exist—but they're secondary. The prototype is what everyone rallies around.

Here's the surprising part: a working prototype is often _more_ descriptive than a written spec while taking _less_ effort to create. You can write a detailed document describing how a date picker should behave—or you can just build it. The prototype answers questions the spec would have left unanswered.

This is increasingly true as products get more dynamic—fluid UX, generative AI, and deep personalization are all hard to define fully on the page.

Design prototypes should be higher fidelity than concept prototypes. Focus on production-level interactions for key flows. You're not exploring options anymore—you're defining the target with enough clarity that there's no room for misinterpretation.

This doesn’t mean PRDs are dead. Often, prototypes work with PRDs to complement the content essential to a good spec: strategic rationale, clear goals, success metrics, prioritization, and timeline.

## Research prototypes — Validation

**When to use:** You've defined a solution. Now you need to test whether customers will actually use it—and get value from it.

The challenge with user research is asking people to predict their own behavior. "Would you use this?" "How would you expect this to work?" Customers try their best, but intentions don't match actions.

AI prototyping changes this. Instead of describing a feature and asking for reactions, you put a working version in front of customers and watch. You're not asking them to imagine—you're observing them use it. That's better feedback.

Research prototypes need to be high-fidelity enough that customers **suspend disbelief**. The prototype should either look like your product or have a look & feel that doesn't distract. And the data matters more than you'd expect. Lorem ipsum won't cut it. You need realistic data that supports real use cases. Sometimes that's the same sample data for every research participant. Other times you plug into user-specific data—their files, their projects, their context. The more real the data, the more real the behavior.

Many prototypes don't need instrumentation. Research prototypes do. Plan to add session capture and logging for key events. That way, you'll get qualitative insights from observing the sessions you conduct and have quantitative data to support your conclusions.

## Technical prototypes — Feasibility

**When to use**: You need to prove you can actually build it—and that it will work the way you intend.

This is where engineers run spikes. Technical prototypes don't need to look good. They need to answer feasibility questions: Can we build this? Will it perform? Does it scale?

This matters more than ever with AI features. Traditional software is predictable—you write code, it executes. AI is probabilistic. You need to know whether you can hit a consistent quality threshold before committing to a direction.

Technical prototypes answer different questions than Concept or Design prototypes. Not "should we build this?" but "can we build this?" Can you get the model to reliably produce the response format you need? Is latency acceptable? Will retrieval quality hold up against real data?

For example, I recently built a technical prototype using clinical data to test whether notes from actual physicians better inform medical responses than a fine-tuned model. The prototype ran on the command line with a built-in AI evaluation process. No UI. No polish. Just output and the metrics to see if we hit the bar.

Technical prototypes are typically built by engineers, sometimes with technical PMs or designers contributing. The output isn't just a tech demo—it's a go/no-go decision.

## The jazz band in action

During any product cycle, you'll reach for some combination of these prototypes—not in a fixed sequence, but as the questions demand:

- Concept prototypes when you're exploring.
    
- Design prototypes when you're defining & aligning.
    
- Research prototypes when you're validating.
    
- Technical prototypes when you're proving feasibility.
    

**The prototype itself is never the point. The decision it enables is.**

## Where prototypes fall short

The speed and polish of AI prototypes creates a new risk: you can align people around the wrong solution faster than ever.

**Don't prototype too early.** Prototyping works best when you know what problem space you're in. If you haven't validated the problem through customer interviews, usage data, or other discovery work, a prototype won't help—you'll build an answer to a question nobody's asking. Prototypes test solutions. They don't identify problems.

**Don't expect prototypes to replace PRDs.** You'll still need goals, success metrics, rationale, and market context. A prototype shows _what_ you're building. A PRD explains _why_—and how you'll know if it worked.

**Don't expect pixel-perfect designs.** At least not yet. Even when your prototype can access your design system, there will be inconsistencies. Plan for a fit-and-finish pass when moving into production.

**Don't expect production code.** AI prototypes are great for brainstorming, defining, aligning, and validating. They help you determine _what_ to build. That's usually where the prototype ends and real engineering begins. The prototype—along with a well-written PRD—gives engineering what they need to build it properly. Let your engineers handle the code. They have their own AI tools to make that faster. Trying to turn a prototype into production code often backfires anyway. The value of prototyping is speed and flexibility. Tying yourself to an established codebase slows you down, limits options, and undermines the role prototyping plays in your process.

**Know the complexity ceiling.** Current AI coding tools have limits. Very complicated features may require working directly with an engineer. This ceiling will rise over time, and there are workarounds—like exposing complex functionality through an API your prototype can call. But for now, recognize when you've hit the limit.

**Don't expect repeatable results.** AI tools produce variations. This works in your favor during concept exploration—you _want_ different directions. It's harder when you need precision in design or research prototypes. Be okay with small deviations. Even with unpredictability, prototypes align teams and validate with customers. And this will improve. Source control and forking help you iterate with more predictability.

None of these limitations change the core value of prototypes. They're still the fastest way to make better product decisions. Just know what they're good for—and what they're not.

## Building a prototyping culture

AI prototyping isn't just a matter of rolling out new tools. It's a shift in how teams build.

Nobody has this fully figured out yet. The loudest LinkedIn takes—"we don't need engineers anymore," "only hire builders," "I replaced my team with AI agents"—are mostly overreach.

**AI prototyping is evolving how we work, not replacing who does the work.**

The critical questions remain the same:

1. What do customers really want?
    
2. How do we best serve them?
    
3. How do we build a durable business around that value?
    

What _has_ changed is the cost of exploring and communicating solutions. Teams that embrace this new way of working share a few traits:

- **They get their hands dirty.** They build prototypes regularly, not occasionally. Skill comes from reps. The teams pulling ahead aren't theorizing about AI tools—they're using them constantly.
    
- **They prototype with purpose.** Every prototype answers a question. If you can't articulate what you're trying to learn, you're not prototyping—you're tinkering.
    
- **They have autonomy to experiment.** Learning requires trying things that might not work. Teams without permission to fail don't build the muscle.
    
- **They aren't territorial.** AI prototyping blurs traditional boundaries. PMs explore UX. Engineers define requirements. Designers validate technical assumptions. The best teams aren't threatened by this—they welcome it. Assembly line handoffs become jazz band collaboration. Riff off each other. Share responsibilities. Work in tighter rhythm than before.
    
- **They have executive support to learn.** This is the most important one. AI prototyping is a skill. Skills take practice. If leadership expects immediate perfection, teams won't experiment. The goal isn't flawless prototypes—it's making prototyping a useful part of how you build products.
    

Teams that embrace this shift build better products faster. Teams that don't are operating at the same old speed while everyone around them accelerates.

## Now, build like you always wanted to

The shift we're describing is fundamental. For decades, the expense of software shaped every aspect of how product teams operated. Artifacts, handoffs, gates, approvals—all designed to build conviction before spending.

That constraint is gone.

Working software is no longer just what you ship at the end. It's how you communicate, make decisions, and validate ideas throughout the entire development process.

Remember where we started: the processes we've inherited weren't designed to help you build great products. They were designed to manage the cost of building the wrong ones.

Now you can focus on what actually matters.

Prototyping is how you get there—not as a phase or a deliverable, but as a default mode for turning ambiguity into clarity. Fluency with prototyping is now as fundamental as writing a clear spec or running a good customer interview.

The opportunity isn't just to work faster. It's to finally build the way we always wanted to—with working software at the center of every decision.
