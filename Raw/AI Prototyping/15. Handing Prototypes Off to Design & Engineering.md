The goal of a prototype is never to ship the prototype itself—it's to unlock everything your team needs to build production software with confidence. In this lesson, you'll learn how to hand prototypes off to design and engineering in a way that accelerates their work rather than creating confusion. We'll cover how to document design intent and interaction decisions, how to communicate what's intentional versus incidental in your prototype, and how to use the prototype as a shared reference point throughout the build process so that the final product stays true to the vision you validated.

## The Prototype Is the Beginning, Not the End

You've built a prototype. Stakeholders are aligned. Customers have validated the concept. Now what?

This is where many teams stumble. The prototype served its purpose — it helped you decide _what_ to build. But the transition from prototype to production is where intention meets reality, and things can go sideways fast.

The goal was never to ship the prototype. It was to unlock everything your team needs to build production software with confidence: a clear vision of the target experience, validated interaction patterns, known edge cases, and a shared understanding of what matters most.

Handing off a prototype isn't like handing off a spec. It's a fundamentally different artifact. It can show things a spec can't — but it can also mislead if the handoff isn't handled thoughtfully. In this lesson, you'll learn how to bridge the gap between prototype and production so that nothing gets lost in translation.

## The polishing trap

The most common mistake I see: PMs trying to make the prototype production-ready. They spend days polishing code that engineering will rewrite from scratch. They agonize over pixel-perfect spacing. They iterate on details that no customer will ever see — because the experience is going to get entirely rebuilt before launch.

It's wasted effort. And worse, it's an effort that could have gone toward building another prototype and testing another assumption.

## Sketches, not paintings

Think about the difference between a sketch and an oil painting. An artist never starts by putting oils directly on a blank canvas. Oil painting is slow, deliberate, and expensive to change. So you start with a pencil sketch — fast, cheap, and easy to revise. The sketch captures the composition, the proportions, and the intent. It tells you whether the painting is worth making. And then — this is the important part — the sketch gets painted over. It's not the final product. It was never meant to be.

Your prototype is that sketch. It captures the flows, the interactions, the intent. It tells your team whether the product is worth building. But it was always meant to be replaced by the fully realized product — just as the pencil lines disappear under layers of oil paint.

The data backs this up. When development consultancy Whitespectre [audited an AI-built prototype(opens in a new tab)](https://www.whitespectre.com/ideas/ai-powered-prototype-to-production-process/) — a Jira evaluation tool a PM had assembled in four hours using v0, Cursor, and ChatGPT — they found that only 30% of the code was salvageable for production. The rest was unusable code. That's not a failure. That's the process working exactly as intended. The prototype compressed weeks of alignment and discovery into a single afternoon.

**The prototype's job is to give the team a running start, not to cross the finish line.**

The handoff is where you pass the conviction you've built — along with the decisions, context, and intent behind it — to the people who will bring it to life in production.

## The New Handoff Problem

Handoffs between product, design, and engineering have always been hard. But AI prototyping has changed the nature of the problem in a way that catches teams off guard.

![](https://cdn5.dcbstatic.com/files/r/e/reforge_docebosaas_com/1790290800/UoviUdyE0A9VxxSvgzh7GQ/scorm/27a4bb54c2353d3917c607717782e2eea18a1559a925dfe2a934ec528bccbdbb/scormcontent/assets/old-vs-new-handoff-problem.svg)

## From too little context to too much context

The old handoff problem was **lost context**. The team would try to get everything down on paper, but something always got lost in the translation. Why was the layout arranged this way? How is this interaction supposed to feel? What should happen in this rare edge case? None of that survived the handoff. Engineers had to reconstruct the thinking from incomplete artifacts, and the result was never quite right.

AI prototyping creates the opposite problem: **excess context with an ambiguous level of detail.** Your prototype shows everything — layout, interactions, data flow, animations, copy, color, spacing. But it doesn't distinguish between the decisions you made deliberately and the decisions the AI made for you. An engineer looking at your prototype can't tell whether the three-step onboarding flow was a carefully considered product decision or something Claude happened to generate.

## More fidelity can mean harder handoffs

This is counterintuitive. You'd expect a higher-fidelity prototype to make things easier. But fidelity without clarity about intent creates noise. And noise slows teams down.

I've seen a minor but telling version of this: copy that the AI generated during prototyping — placeholder text that nobody reviewed carefully — shipping all the way to production.

Copy is a funny thing. Most of the time, it's invisible, especially for teams that are in their own product all day long, every day. Yet when a user is lost, copy becomes the most critical thing on the page to help them find their way. Poor AI-generated copy is a small example of a bigger pattern: the AI fills in hundreds of small decisions, and most don't matter until suddenly one does.

## The stakeholder dimension

There's also a stakeholder problem. A designer builds a prototype in a few hours that connects to a real API — and the immediate response from leadership is: "When are we shipping this?" The prototype looks so complete that executives assume it's nearly production-ready. Yet there are often critical missing pieces — edge case handling, security compliance, instrumentation, AI evaluations — that are invisible in a demo but essential to launch.

The problem isn't that your team can't see what you're building. It's that they can see _too much_ — and the delta between "just a prototype" and "production ready" is murky. Some teams have resorted to sharing screen recordings instead of live demos, just to manage expectations.

## The Prototype Fidelity Spectrum

So how do you decide what role the prototype should play in the handoff? There's no single right answer — and that's the point.

![](https://cdn5.dcbstatic.com/files/r/e/reforge_docebosaas_com/1790290800/UoviUdyE0A9VxxSvgzh7GQ/scorm/27a4bb54c2353d3917c607717782e2eea18a1559a925dfe2a934ec528bccbdbb/scormcontent/assets/prototype-fidelity-spectru.svg)

We can think about prototype fidelity along a spectrum from least to most informative:

- **Living wireframe.** A visual reference — a sketch that shows layout, flow, and basic interactions. Design and engineering use it to understand the shape of the experience, then build from scratch.
    
- **Functional specification.** Detailed enough to serve as part of the product spec. Key interactions, states, and flows are demonstrated rather than described. The PRD handles strategy and edge cases; the prototype handles "what does it look and feel like."
    
- **Rough draft.** The prototype's code becomes a detailed reference — essentially a prompt — that engineers use as context when writing production code with AI-assisted development tools.
    
- **Working software.** The prototype code, with hardening and review from engineering, becomes the production software itself. This is the exception, not the rule — and we'll cover when it makes sense later in this lesson.
    

As we discussed in [The New Product Development Lifecycle(opens in a new tab)](https://edu.reforge.com/learn/courses/7165/ai-prototyping/lessons/14114:6754/introduction-to-prototyping), product teams are moving away from rigid, linear processes — the assembly-line model where artifacts get passed sequentially from one function to the next. We're moving toward something more like a jazz band, where the process is fluid and adapts to the needs of the moment. The role your prototype plays in the handoff should be equally fluid. But fluidity doesn't mean ambiguity — you need to decide where on this spectrum your prototype will land _before_ you build it.

This key decision prevents the polishing trap. If you've agreed upfront that the prototype is essentially a working wireframe, then you don't need to waste time making it pixel-perfect. If, on the other hand, you've agreed that the prototype's code will serve as a rough draft for engineering, you'll need to invest in code quality from the start.

Document this decision early in the process. It's one of the most important pieces of context your team needs when interpreting a prototype.

## What Design and Engineering Actually Need

Designers and engineers look at prototypes through different lenses. Understanding those lenses will help you build a better handoff document.

## The designer's perspective

Designers see two things in your prototype: **intent** and **debt.** Intent is the stuff you got right — the flows, the hierarchy, the interaction patterns that work. Debt is everything else — inconsistent spacing, off-brand colors, components that don't match the design system, accessibility gaps. Designers expect debt. It's not a problem unless you pretend it's not there.

What designers need from you: a clear distinction between what's intentional and what's scaffolding, the reasoning behind key decisions, and permission to improve. Don't hand off a prototype and say, "build exactly this."

## The engineer's perspective

Engineers evaluate scope, data model implications, edge cases, performance at scale, and post-launch operability — instrumentation, monitoring, and AI evaluations needed after launch.

What engineers need from you: a clear map of what the prototype covers and what it doesn't, the underlying customer insights and strategic goals that led to this solution, and priority guidance for what must ship first.

## Documenting Your Prototype for Handoff

A good handoff document bridges the gap between "here's a prototype" and "here's everything you need to build this." It doesn't need to be long, but it does need to be clear.

![](https://cdn5.dcbstatic.com/files/r/e/reforge_docebosaas_com/1790290800/UoviUdyE0A9VxxSvgzh7GQ/scorm/27a4bb54c2353d3917c607717782e2eea18a1559a925dfe2a934ec528bccbdbb/scormcontent/assets/anatomy-handoff-document.svg)

## 1. Experience overview

A short narrative walkthrough of the core experience. Write it from the user's perspective:

_"A team lead opens the dashboard and sees this week's key metrics at a glance. They notice a spike in error rate and click into the details view, which shows a breakdown by service. They filter to the affected service, identify the problematic endpoint, and create an incident ticket directly from the dashboard."_

This helps the team understand the _story_, not just the screens.

## 2. Flow-by-flow breakdown

For each critical flow, document:

|**Element**|**What to include**|
|---|---|
|Flow name|A clear, descriptive name (e.g., "Incident creation from dashboard")|
|Entry point|How the user gets here|
|Key interactions|Step-by-step actions and expected outcomes|
|Intentional decisions|Design choices that should be preserved and why|
|Known gaps|What the prototype doesn't handle that production must|
|Edge cases|Empty states, errors, boundary conditions|

## 3. Intentional vs. incidental

This is worth its own section — and it's worth being thorough. Walk through the prototype and categorize every notable element. Here's what this might look like for a trip itinerary feature:

- ✅ **Intentional — preserve this:**
    
    - Day-by-day tab navigation as the primary way to browse the itinerary (not a long scrolling list or calendar view)
        
    - Card-based layout for itinerary items, with image on the left and details on the right
        
    - Visual distinction between activity types (Stay, Dining, Market) via category badges on each card
        
    - Collapsible comments system on each card — comments are per-item, not per-day
        
    - Trip header with cover photo, destination, date range, and traveler avatars as the top-level context
        
    - "Free time" as a distinct, lighter-weight card type between structured activities
        
    - Ratings and review counts on venue cards to signal credibility
        
- ⚠️ **Incidental — feel free to change:**
    
    - Card dimensions and image aspect ratios
        
    - Tag styling and colors (e.g., "Luxury," "Boutique," "River View" pills)
        
    - Exact layout of the day tab bar (pill style, spacing, chevron separators)
        
    - Cover photo treatment and text overlay positioning
        
    - Typography, font sizes, and spacing throughout
        
    - Star icon and rating display format
        
    - "Free time" suggestion chips ("Relax in a park," "Take photos around town") — these were AI-generated
        
- ❓ **Open — needs a decision:**
    
    - Should travelers be able to reorder or drag-and-drop itinerary items within a day?
        
    - What happens when two travelers are editing comments on the same card simultaneously? Should we show a typing indicator?
        
    - How does the experience adapt on mobile — do day tabs become a dropdown or horizontal scroll?
        
    - Should there be notifications when someone comments on an item you've interacted with?
        

## 4. What the prototype doesn't cover

Be explicit about gaps. Engineers and designers will assume the prototype is comprehensive unless you tell them otherwise:

- **Missing states:** Loading, empty, error, permission-denied, first-time user experience
    
- **Missing scale:** How does this work with 1,000 items instead of 10? Is it paginated? Infinite scroll?
    
- **Missing platforms:** Mobile, tablet, accessibility, internationalization
    
- **Missing security:** Authentication, authorization, role-based access, data privacy
    
- **Mocked integrations:** Any data or services that are hardcoded or simulated in the prototype
    

## 5. Using prototype code as a reference

Your prototype's code can be a valuable reference — but frame it correctly. It's not production code. It's a **communication tool.** Engineers should use it to understand behavior and intent, not as a foundation to build on. Prototype code is optimized for speed and flexibility. Production code needs to optimize for maintainability, performance, security, and scale.

That said, prototype code can be genuinely useful for:

- **Demonstrating interaction logic.** How filtering works, how state transitions happen, and what conditions trigger specific behaviors.
    
- **Showing data structures.** The shape of the data the UI expects can inform API and database design.
    
- **Illustrating component hierarchy.** How the UI is broken into components can inform the production component architecture (even if the implementation differs).
    
- **AI behavior.** If your prototype includes prompts, model configurations, or AI workflows, these are often directly transferable — or at least a strong starting point.
    

The key distinction: use prototype code as _inspiration and reference_, not as a _foundation to build on_.

## The Handoff Conversation

Documentation is necessary but not sufficient. The handoff _conversation_ is where alignment actually happens.

## The handoff toolkit

In practice, a good handoff combines several artifacts:

- **A PRD.** Provides the strategic context that the prototype can't communicate on its own — as we covered in [Integrating Prototypes Into Your PRD and Product Review Process(opens in a new tab)](https://edu.reforge.com/learn/courses/7165/ai-prototyping/lessons/14117:6760/integrating-prototyping-into-your-process).
    
- **A prototype walkthrough.** A short Loom video where you walk through the key flows, explain what's intentional versus scaffolding, and flag the open questions. This is often the single most valuable handoff artifact. It's low-effort, high-context, and people can rewatch it.
    
- **A good ticket.** A well-structured Jira or Linear ticket that breaks the work into actionable pieces, with links back to the PRD and prototype.
    
- **A product review.** Walk through the prototype with your design and engineering leads. Let them interact with it while you narrate the intent behind key decisions.
    

You may not need all of these depending on your process. But with this combination, your team may not have every question answered — they'll know enough to know which questions to ask. That's the real goal.

## Running a handoff session

Set aside 60–90 minutes with the designer and tech lead (at minimum). Structure the session:

1. **Walk through the prototype together (20-30 min).** Let them interact with it. Narrate the intentional decisions. Answer questions in real time.
    
2. **Review the handoff document (15-20 min).** Go through the flow-by-flow breakdown. Confirm that the "intentional vs. incidental" distinctions make sense to everyone.
    
3. **Discuss what's missing (15-20 min).** This is where engineers and designers flag gaps, edge cases, and concerns. Take notes. Update the handoff document.
    
4. **Align on approach and sequence (10-15 min).** What gets built first? Are there technical spikes needed? Does design need to do a full pass before engineering starts, or can they work in parallel?
    

## Questions to expect (and prepare for)

**Designers will ask:**

- "Are these design choices (spacing, layout, fonts, colors, etc.) intentional or just how the tool rendered it?"
    
- "Did you explore other layouts for this section?"
    
- "What happens on mobile?"
    
- "Should this match our existing component for [similar pattern]?"
    

**Engineers will ask:**

- "Where does this data come from?"
    
- "What happens when this list has hundreds of items?"
    
- "Is this a new API endpoint or can we extend an existing one?"
    
- "What's the expected behavior if the request fails?"
    
- “What parts of this experience do we want to instrument for analytics?”
    

Having thought through these questions in advance makes the handoff dramatically smoother.

## After the handoff

Your prototype doesn't stop being useful once the conversation ends. It serves as a reference throughout the build — for design reviews, engineering edge cases, scope discussions, and QA. Keep it accessible and pin the link somewhere visible: the project channel, the PRD, the project tracker. When it's easy to find, people actually use it. When it's buried in a Slack thread from two weeks ago, it gets forgotten.

## Tailoring the Handoff to Your Team

The right handoff process depends partly on your team and where their strengths lie. A good prototyping process fills in the gaps others want but also leaves open permission to rethink, redesign, and rebuild where talented humans can achieve what AI cannot.

When your partners have strong opinions and deep investment in the product, you can hand off early with the essentials — they'll elevate the work. When the team is less familiar with the problem space or less engaged in shaping the solution, invest more in the handoff artifacts: more annotations, more detailed walkthroughs, more explicit guidance about what's intentional.

## Working with strong, opinionated designers

A strong designer doesn't want your polished prototype. They want to understand your _intent_ — then rebuild it properly in their design system.

This goes back to the sketch metaphor. Give a great painter a detailed sketch with clear annotations about composition and mood, and they'll produce something better than you imagined. Give them an amateur, half-finished painting, and they'll need to figure out what to keep, what to ignore, and what to rethink.

When your design partner has a mature design system and strong craft skills, hand off the intent, not the interface. Share the prototype as a reference, give them permission to redesign — preserving the key decisions while elevating the execution.

One reason this matters: AI prototypes routinely use incorrect colors, non-standard typography, wrong spacing, and unapproved components. Without explicit design token integration, AI builds from general patterns rather than your organization's specific design logic. An accessibility audit of AI-generated demo sites found over two hundred WCAG violations on a single site — missing alt text, failing contrast ratios, and semantically meaningless markup. A strong designer will catch all of this. But they need to know they have permission to redesign, not just polish.

## Working with product-minded engineers

Product-minded engineers don't just want to know _what_ to build. They want to understand _why_ you made the tradeoffs you made, so they can make good calls on the hundred small decisions that come up during implementation.

For these engineers, the most valuable handoff artifact isn't the prototype itself. It's the underlying thinking. Why did you go with a three-step flow instead of a single page? Why is this specific call-to-action above the fold?

When your engineering partner has strong product instincts, hand off earlier rather than later. You don't need a fully baked prototype — they'll fill in the gaps productively. The same is true when you're working on a technically sophisticated system where engineering considerations need to inform the design from the start.

## When Prototypes Go to Production

The default should be clear: a prototype is a sketch, and sketches get replaced. But there are cases where the prototype can legitimately become production software — or get very close.

The prerequisite is typically one of two things: either a **small, forgiving audience with close relationships**, or **strong engineering infrastructure and review processes**. And even when shipping makes sense, do it deliberately — the decision to harden a prototype into production code should be an explicit choice, not a default.

![](https://cdn5.dcbstatic.com/files/r/e/reforge_docebosaas_com/1790290800/UoviUdyE0A9VxxSvgzh7GQ/scorm/27a4bb54c2353d3917c607717782e2eea18a1559a925dfe2a934ec528bccbdbb/scormcontent/assets/prototypes-to-production.svg)

## Case 1: Zero-to-one products with early adopters

When you're going from zero to one, you may be able to vibe-code a full-stack product and put it directly in front of customers. Your audience is small — early adopters or design partners who are open to experiences that aren't perfect. They're not expecting polish. They're expecting to help shape something new.

In this context, the prototype _is_ the product for a while. You're getting feedback not just on usability but on the business itself — whether there's real demand, whether the value proposition resonates, whether people will pay.

## Case 2: Internal tools

Internal tools share the same characteristics: a small, known user base with close relationships and a high tolerance for imperfection. The feedback loop is tight, and nobody expects the internal admin tool to feel like a consumer app.

But here's what makes this case exciting: prototypes can dramatically elevate internal tools that would otherwise never get engineering investment. A PM at a HealthTech company I'm working with recently prototyped and launched several internal tools — including a live visit dashboard showing all patient visits in progress, with start times, join times, and an indicator for late arrivals. The team can now spot providers who are consistently late to visits in real time. That's a back-office system turned into a team-wide capability, built and shipped as a prototype.

## Case 3: Mature tooling and review processes

The third case is different. It's not about a forgiving audience who may overlook flaws in vibe-coded experiences. It's about having the engineering infrastructure to turn vibe-coded experiences into production software.

Companies are going to great lengths to make this possible: democratizing access to the production codebase, getting teams up and running on tools like Claude Code and Cursor, and creating documentation that helps both people and AI understand how to work within the codebase. They're making design systems and component libraries available so that AI-generated code leverages established patterns rather than inventing its own. On top of this tooling, they have engineering processes to review code before it goes into production. A prototype written on top of this infrastructure has the scaffolding to go from sketch to fully working product.

This matters because AI-generated code carries real risks. A recent study found that 45% of AI-generated code contains security vulnerabilities. The code works — it demonstrates the right behavior — but it's doing things under the hood that go against good security practices. These are exactly the issues that good engineering infrastructure helps prevent from getting into the code in the first place, and helps identify if they do make it in.

Importantly, though, mature tooling and processes don't mean that prototyping-as-sketching loses its value. There's a real advantage in creating a prototype you know is disposable from the outset. You can work quickly, make changes freely, chase opportunities, and treat it as part of the journey rather than the destination. Often, it's not worth weighing down a prototype with production concerns until you've proven you're working on the right problem to begin with.

## The PM as Curator

Throughout this course, we've returned to a foundational idea: the product development lifecycle is being reshaped by AI. In [From Prompt to Prototype in Minutes(opens in a new tab)](https://edu.reforge.com/learn/courses/7165/ai-prototyping/lessons/14114:6754/introduction-to-prototyping), we talked about how the cost of building working software is collapsing. In [The New Product Development Lifecycle(opens in a new tab)](https://edu.reforge.com/learn/courses/7165/ai-prototyping/lessons/14114:6754/introduction-to-prototyping), we explored how that changes the process — from assembly line to jazz band, from sequential handoffs to collaborative loops. Over the course, you learned to build prototypes that communicate ideas, test assumptions, and generate conviction. In this week's lessons, you've seen how prototypes integrate into PRDs, customer research, and the handoff to design and engineering.

Now let's bring it full circle.

**AI prototyping is raising the floor.**

Teams can build more, test more, and iterate more than ever before. Features that might not have made the roadmap — the nice-to-haves that delight customers but never justified the engineering investment — are now within reach. We can ship better products by spending more time polishing and honing. We can ship the _right_ products by having more frequent conversations with customers.

But there's a counterbalancing force. When building is cheap, it's tempting to ship everything. AI-coded products can feel like AI slop if you don't take the time to focus on the user experience. You can ship a lot of features at lower quality that may not be as useful or as needed. The product gets bloated over time.

This is why the PM's role as curator matters more than ever.

In a world where anyone can generate features, the PM's job isn't to build the most — it's to ensure the team builds only what matters.

**Part of building faster is being able to throw things out.** You went down a path that wasn't a good one, but the sunk cost was low, so you threw out that work and moved on. That's not failure. That's the process working.

The best product teams won't be the ones that ship the most features. They'll be the ones who learn the fastest and ship only what matters. They'll prototype broadly to explore the solution space, test rigorously to separate signal from noise, and hand off with clarity so that what gets built in production is grounded in real customer insight and strategic intent.

We can finally build the way we always wanted to. Not faster for the sake of faster — but faster in service of learning, conviction, and craft. The prototype is your most powerful tool in that pursuit. Use it to sketch. Use it to learn. Use it to convince. And then hand it off — knowing that the best is yet to come when your team brings it to life.
